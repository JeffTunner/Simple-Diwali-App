The Festival of Lights

A journey through the timeless legends of Diwali

🪔 What is Diwali?

Diwali, also known as Deepavali, is one of the most important and widely celebrated festivals in the Indian subcontinent and among the global Indian diaspora. The name comes from the Sanskrit words Deepa (lamp) and Avali (row), meaning “a row of lights.”

It is a five-day festival that usually falls between mid-October and mid-November, according to the Hindu lunar calendar. At its heart, Diwali celebrates the eternal victory of light over darkness, knowledge over ignorance, and good over evil.

🏹 The Return of Lord Rama

In North India, Diwali is most closely associated with the return of Lord Rama to Ayodhya after 14 years of exile.

According to the epic Ramayana, Prince Rama was sent into exile by his father King Dasharatha to honour a promise made to Queen Kaikeyi. Accompanied by his devoted wife Sita and loyal brother Lakshmana, Rama lived in the forests. During this time, the demon king Ravana of Lanka abducted Sita.

With the help of Hanuman and an army of vanaras (monkey warriors), Rama crossed the ocean, defeated Ravana in a great battle, and rescued Sita. After completing the full term of exile, Rama, Sita and Lakshmana returned to Ayodhya. The people of the city illuminated every home and street with rows of earthen lamps to welcome their rightful king — a tradition that continues as the lighting of diyas on Diwali.

⚔️ Krishna & the Defeat of Narakasura

In many parts of South India, Goa, Maharashtra and Assam, the day before the main Diwali night is celebrated as Naraka Chaturdashi, commemorating Lord Krishna’s victory over the demon Narakasura.

Narakasura (also called Bhaumasura) was the son of Bhudevi (Mother Earth) and Varaha, the boar avatar of Vishnu. Despite his divine origin, he grew arrogant and tyrannical. He stole the earrings of Aditi (mother of the gods), seized Varuna’s umbrella, and imprisoned 16,000 women.

Lord Krishna, accompanied by his consort Satyabhama (an incarnation of Bhudevi), attacked Narakasura’s fortress at Pragjyotishapura. After defeating the demon Mura and then Narakasura himself, Krishna liberated the captives. It is said that the dying Narakasura requested that his death be remembered with lights and celebration rather than mourning — a wish that was granted.

🪷 Goddess Lakshmi & the Churning of the Ocean

Diwali is also the night when Goddess Lakshmi, the deity of wealth, prosperity and good fortune, is worshipped. According to the Puranas, during the great cosmic event known as Samudra Manthan (the churning of the ocean of milk), many divine treasures emerged.

Among them was Goddess Lakshmi, who chose Lord Vishnu as her eternal consort. On Diwali night, devotees clean their homes, light diyas, and offer prayers to invite Lakshmi into their lives, believing that the goddess visits only pure and illuminated homes.

📅 The Five Days of Diwali

Dhanteras – The first day is dedicated to Dhanvantari, the god of Ayurveda, and to the purchase of gold, silver or new utensils for prosperity.

Naraka Chaturdashi / Chhoti Diwali – The second day remembers the defeat of Narakasura. People take ritual oil baths before sunrise.

Lakshmi Puja – The main day of Diwali. Homes are illuminated, prayers are offered to Lakshmi and Ganesha, and families gather for sweets and fireworks.

Govardhan Puja / Annakut – In many regions this day commemorates Krishna lifting Govardhan Hill to protect the people of Vrindavan. Mountains of food (Annakut) are offered.

Bhai Dooj – The final day celebrates the bond between brothers and sisters, similar to Raksha Bandhan.

✨ The Eternal Message

Beyond the stories and rituals, Diwali carries a universal message: no matter how dark the night, light will always return. Whether through the lamps of Ayodhya, the victory of Krishna, or the grace of Lakshmi, the festival reminds us to kindle the light of knowledge, compassion and hope within ourselves and share it with the world.
